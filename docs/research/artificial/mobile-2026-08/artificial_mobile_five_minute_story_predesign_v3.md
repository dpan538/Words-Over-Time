## Principal research question

How can *artificial* describe skilled making, performed feeling, manufactured experience, bodily help, and uncertain human-like capacities without collapsing into a synonym for *fake*?

## Strongest supported finding

Across the selected sources, *artificial* names distinct relationships rather than one degree of falseness: making and performance, bodily support and replacement, and modeled or speculative human-like activity.

The clearest quantitative contrast sits inside the selected human-function evidence table:

- all 10 bodily-support and functional-replacement examples use established terms;
- none of the 5 speculative-extension examples uses an established term.

Two supporting method indicators—19 of 25 examples with stronger source support (`76%`) and 16 of 25 describing currently established uses (`64%`)—belong in expandable evidence detail rather than the headline. All percentages describe this selected 25-example table, not English, medicine, technology, or public opinion.

## Immediate semantic correction

The latter includes companion, consciousness, and AGI material. It must not be presented as 11 already realized simulated abilities. Every proposed figure, summary, accessible description, and closing sentence in this revision uses the `6 + 5` split.

### Opening

> Most people meet “artificial” as the opposite of natural—or as a warning that something is fake. The sources here show a more divided history: skilled making, manufactured experience, social performance, bodily help, and simulated or imagined human capacities.

### Beat 1 — early meanings

> “Artificial” did not begin with one meaning. Technical skill and made things appear beside a separate branch of feigned feeling and not-genuine things.

Essential caveat beside the marks:

> These are selected historical sources, not a claim about the word's single earliest use.

### Beat 2 — source support

> Eleven statements about the word's history were checked: four directly support the finding, four support it indirectly, one supplies definition context, and two are not used in the conclusion.

### Beat 3 — phrase spread

> Forty-eight selected “artificial …” phrases reach across the body, mind, materials, senses, and social life. Each dot is one exact written phrase, not a frequency count.

### Beat 4 — social performance

> “Artificial tears” and “artificial manners” show the word judging performed feeling and social behavior. The other phrases here show where the study looked next; they are questions, not proof by themselves.

### Beat 5 — manufactured experience

> Ninety selected comparison terms show what the study examined across optical devices, sound, scenes, broadcasting, digital images, and authenticity. This is a comparison map, not a popularity ranking or a measured historical sequence.

### Beat 6 — differences between related words

> Something can be artificial and still be real: one source describes an artificial tsunami that is still a real event. Artificial skin can be made to look more realistic, while an FCC ruling describes voice cloning as artificially simulating a human voice.

### Beat 7 — distrust in context

> Fourteen dated examples show distrust attaching to coloring, ingredients, processing, and claims that a product contains “no artificial” additions. These examples do not measure public opinion.

### Beat 8 — support and replacement

> Support keeps a bodily process going. Replacement stands in for a missing part or function. The selected sources contain five examples of each, and all ten use established terms.

### Beat 9 — continuation

> Four source examples cover three terms. Artificial insemination and IVF are established interventions; two sources describe artificial-womb work that remains animal or preclinical.

### Beat 10 — modeled versus speculative

> Six examples describe modeled processes already present in dictionaries, policy, or practice. Five extend the word toward companion, consciousness, or artificial general intelligence claims whose status remains emerging or speculative.

## Continuous-field composition rules

- One black surface and one subtle grid extend from the top navigation through Close Finding and the footer.
- Movements are separated by density, 48–72 px breathing intervals, and at most one shared hairline—not boxes, cards, grey panels, or white-page cuts.
- Major interactive geometry receives 400–520 px of vertical field at 390 px. It is never compressed into a half-width mobile column.
- Similar forms are separated: source path → segmented state → phrase tree → text constellation → media map → paired relation plots → source atlas → human sphere → staircase → dot matrix.
- No two consecutive movements use the same plot grammar.
- Every luminous data nucleus is a true circle in every resting and rotated state. Halos are decoration tied to the same node, never additional data points.
- Lines show group membership or source relations only. Visible notes state that position, depth, and connector length are visual layout, not quantities.
- Key visible copy is normally 15–17 px; direct labels are at least 13 px. Terminal texture does not justify unreadable 7–10 px public text.

## Text–chart motion contract

Each movement owns one chart state and one emphasized phrase. They enter together:

1. Server rendering shows complete content and final geometry.
2. After hydration, Intersection Observer arms a movement when roughly 15% is visible.
3. The chart grows once over 600–1,000 ms in a declared data order.
4. The matching word receives one soft luminance pulse as its final data group resolves.
5. Returning to the movement does not replay automatically.
6. A true tap on a non-control chart area or a visible 44 px `Replay` control redraws the same marks without changing data, order, or count.
7. Drag, scroll, pointer cancellation, or pinch never triggers replay.
8. Reduced-motion and no-JavaScript modes show the final chart and copy immediately.

There is no rapid blinking, random particle generation, idle rotation, scroll-jacked animation, or animation whose absence hides meaning.

## Single-axis 3D and Safari touch contract

The phrase tree and human-functions sphere use projected SVG/DOM geometry, not a free camera and not all-axis rotation.

- Rotation is yaw only, clamped to approximately `−45° … +45°`.
- `touch-action: pan-y pinch-zoom` remains on the chart surface.
- A gesture begins in `pending`. Horizontal rotation locks only after at least 10 px movement and when horizontal travel exceeds vertical travel by at least 8 px.
- A vertical or ambiguous gesture becomes page scrolling; it does not capture the pointer or change yaw.
- Pointer capture begins only after horizontal intent is established.
- `pointercancel` rolls back to the gesture's starting yaw and never announces or replays.
- `lostpointercapture` performs idempotent cleanup only.
- The left and right 24 px visual-viewport edge zones never capture rotation, preserving Safari navigation gestures.
- A completed drag suppresses the following click so it cannot trigger redraw.
- Controls provide left, reset, and right with consistent steps and disabled boundary states.
- Long-press selection/callout is disabled only inside the rotator; page text and links remain selectable.
- During dragging, expensive glow is simplified; no idle animation or per-point `will-change` is used.
- `dvh` and scroll-timeline animation are not baseline dependencies; stable `svh`, fixed chart geometry, and Intersection Observer avoid toolbar-driven hit-box shifts.
