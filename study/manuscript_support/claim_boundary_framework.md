# Claim-boundary framework

## Core proposition

Visual communication is not only a display layer for linguistic evidence. It structures what readers believe they are allowed to infer from evidence.

## Six evidence roles

| Role | Reader question | Claim boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signal | What changed in the visible corpus trace? | Frequency is source-specific visibility, not importance. |
| Attestation | What supports earliest known use? | Earliest attestation is not necessarily first historical use. |
| Variant | Which forms are grouped or separated? | Variant grouping is editorial and must be declared. |
| Context | What examples show semantic use? | Examples are partial evidence, not total usage. |
| Boundary | What can the page not claim? | Absence, OCR gaps, genre bias, and corpus limits remain visible. |
| Rights | What can be reused or quoted? | Attribution and copyright limits are part of the evidence system. |

## Reader-study expectations

- E1: The evidence-grid condition will reduce frequency misinterpretation.
- E2: The evidence-grid condition will improve source-transparency ratings.
- E3: Boundary and Rights columns may increase perceived difficulty while improving claim awareness.
- E4: Design readers may focus on hierarchy and layout; language-history readers may focus on source validity.

## Manuscript phrasing

Words Over Time shows how visual communication can make semantic change legible without erasing the boundaries between frequency, attestation, context, interpretation, and rights.
