# Style Positioning

Use this reference when the user asks for a visual style, a drawing direction, a historical influence, or a design language. The goal is to help the agent locate the design source quickly without copying a protected project identity.

Style is a research decision. Before choosing colors, shapes, or illustration technique, define the style's provenance and its relationship to the content.

## Style Positioning Brief

For every substantial design, define:

- Region or school: country, city, institution, movement, or design culture.
- Historical period: approximate years or era.
- Medium: print poster, newspaper, atlas, catalog, exhibition panel, web interface, technical manual, slide deck, dashboard, or archive.
- Drawing bias: diagrammatic, typographic, cartographic, pictorial, technical, editorial, expressive, or minimal.
- Evidence fit: why this style helps the content rather than merely decorating it.
- Adaptation rule: what to borrow as method and what not to copy as identity.

## Regional And Historical Anchors

Use these as broad orientation points. They are not templates.

### Swiss / International Typographic Style, 1950s-1970s

Best for: systematic comparison, civic information, evidence hierarchy, source-led editorial systems.

Signals:

- Modular grids.
- Sans serif typography.
- Asymmetrical balance.
- Strong alignment and rules.
- Limited functional color.
- Photography or diagrams used as evidence, not ornament.

Drawing bias:

- Diagrammatic, typographic, restrained, structural.

Avoid:

- Treating Swiss style as empty minimalism.
- Making everything gray and sterile.

### Ulm / Systems Design, 1950s-1960s

Best for: method diagrams, interface logic, product systems, evidence contracts, taxonomies.

Signals:

- Rational structure.
- Programmatic components.
- Diagrams and systems thinking.
- Design as epistemology rather than surface.

Drawing bias:

- Technical, modular, procedural.

Avoid:

- Over-intellectualizing simple content.

### Dutch Information Design, 1960s-1990s

Best for: public information, maps, civic systems, clear signage, data explanation.

Signals:

- Direct typography.
- Bright but controlled color.
- Practical hierarchy.
- Strong icon and diagram culture.

Drawing bias:

- Civic, diagrammatic, accessible.

Avoid:

- Over-playful color that weakens evidence.

### British Editorial / Newspaper Infographics, 1970s-2000s

Best for: explanatory journalism, timelines, public events, political/economic data, annotated diagrams.

Signals:

- Clear headlines and captions.
- Strong annotation.
- Mixed text, chart, and illustration.
- Pragmatic pacing.

Drawing bias:

- Editorial, explanatory, annotated.

Avoid:

- Too much newspaper nostalgia when the artifact is not journalistic.

### French Structural / Cultural Poster Traditions, 1950s-1980s

Best for: cultural essays, exhibitions, theory-heavy visual systems, image-text tension.

Signals:

- Strong conceptual framing.
- Image/text juxtaposition.
- Elegant restraint or sharp graphic wit.
- Poster-like argument.

Drawing bias:

- Conceptual, editorial, semiotic.

Avoid:

- Ambiguity that hides the evidence.

### Japanese Editorial And Diagrammatic Design, 1960s-2000s

Best for: dense layouts, catalog systems, technical/editorial hybrids, layered annotation.

Signals:

- High information density.
- Careful micro-typography.
- Modular page logic.
- Strong relationship between text blocks, labels, and diagrams.

Drawing bias:

- Dense, precise, layered, crafted.

Avoid:

- Shrinking type below readable thresholds.

### Bauhaus / Constructivist / Early Modernist, 1910s-1930s

Best for: historical modernity, machine-age themes, manifestos, geometric systems.

Signals:

- Geometric composition.
- Diagonal energy.
- Primary colors or stark contrast.
- Photomontage or typographic force.

Drawing bias:

- Geometric, dynamic, ideological.

Avoid:

- Using revolutionary style for neutral evidence unless the content calls for it.

### American Mid-Century Information Graphics, 1940s-1970s

Best for: science communication, public education, technical explainers, institutional reports.

Signals:

- Clear diagrams.
- Friendly but disciplined illustration.
- Strong labeling.
- Educational pacing.

Drawing bias:

- Didactic, technical, accessible.

Avoid:

- Retro styling that turns evidence into nostalgia.

## Drawing Style Bias

Choose the drawing bias according to the evidence task:

- Diagrammatic: relationships, systems, flows, taxonomies.
- Cartographic: geography, territory, spatial distribution, movement.
- Typographic: language, classification, labels, editorial voice.
- Technical: mechanisms, infrastructure, process, engineering.
- Pictorial: objects, scenes, public education, human-facing explanation.
- Archival: historical documents, fragments, scans, provenance.
- Expressive: atmosphere, conflict, subjective interpretation; use only when evidence boundaries remain clear.
- Minimal: fast comparison, sparse signal, interface clarity; avoid when it erases context.

## Style Fit Questions

Before rendering:

- Does the chosen style make the evidence easier to inspect?
- Does it clarify source boundaries or blur them?
- Does the historical reference match the subject's tone and period?
- Is the style being used as method, not costume?
- What visual identity must not be copied?
- What should be newly designed for this subject?

## Output Format

When asked for style direction, return:

- Style source: region/school, period, medium.
- Why it fits the content.
- Grid implication.
- Typography implication.
- Color implication.
- Drawing bias.
- What to avoid.
- A short prompt-ready visual direction.
